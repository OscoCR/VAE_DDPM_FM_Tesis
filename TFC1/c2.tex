\chapter{Background and State of the Art}

\section{Application Context}

This section describes the use of synthetic images to address data scarcity in agriculture and its limits, and the application that motivates this work, plant counting from drone images. It also places the thesis in the research line of the group.

\subsection{Synthetic images from few data}

Synthetic images have been used to enrich the training data of plant-related tasks. Images generated with GANs improved the detection and counting of sorghum panicles in UAV imagery \cite{Cai2022}. Diffusion models offer greater training stability and image quality than GANs \cite{Hu2025}, and have been used to augment plant disease images \cite{Muhammad2023}, to improve vineyard shoot detection \cite{Hirahara2025}, and to improve detection in few-shot settings \cite{Fang2024}; a Flow Matching (FM) model that generates pest and disease images together with their masks improved segmentation \cite{Kong2026}; and a detector for onboard UAV use has been trained on a synthetic dataset \cite{Bernardo2023}.

\subsection{The data problem in agriculture}

In agriculture, collecting and annotating images is hard for three reasons.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Few and homogeneous data.} A campaign covers a few flights over a single field, with one type of soil, specific growth stages, and similar illumination, so even thousands of frames show little variability. Small, homogeneous UAV datasets are typical of precision agriculture \cite{Gonzalez2026}.
  \item \textbf{Limited transfer between domains.} A detector trained in one domain does not necessarily work in another. In maize seedling counting from drone orthomosaics, no model trained on out-of-distribution data reached acceptable counting accuracy, whereas models trained on in-domain data did so with 60 to 130 annotated images \cite{Bumbaca2025}. Domain shift is also a recognized obstacle in plant counting \cite{Ayalew2020}.
  \item \textbf{Manual and costly annotation.} Pixel-level annotation of agricultural images by an expert is extremely time-consuming \cite{Bah2018}, annotating small and dense objects is burdensome \cite{Ayalew2020}, and the labor-intensive ground truthing of UAV images is a bottleneck for the detection and counting of sorghum panicles \cite{Cai2022}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limits of synthetic images with few data}

The use of synthetic images has limits that are especially relevant when the generator is trained with few and homogeneous data. First, diversity: a generator learns the distribution of its training images, so it can produce new instances of the scenes it saw, but it cannot be expected to produce conditions it never saw, such as another soil, another illumination, or another field. It may also reproduce its training images instead of producing new ones: latent diffusion models trained on CT, MR, and X-ray datasets showed a high degree of memorization of their training data, higher than that of autoencoders and GANs, even though their synthesis quality was better \cite{Dar2024}. Second, usefulness: synthetic images do not always match real ones; a classifier trained only with synthetic brain MRI slices reached an AUC of 0.779 against 0.869 with real data \cite{Fallah2026}, and with Stable Diffusion on small datasets the results were promising only for some classes \cite{Deng2023}. Third, labels: generated images do not come with annotations, so using them to train a detector requires labeling them, for example with pseudo-labels \cite{Fallas2025Pseudo} or by generating images and masks jointly \cite{Kong2026}. Whether a generator trained on few and homogeneous images produces real diversity is therefore an open question, and it depends on the latent space in which the generator works, which motivates this work.

\subsection{Plant counting and detection}

The application that motivates this work is plant counting from drone images. Plant density is one of the main factors that determine yield, but measuring it by hand is impractical in large areas \cite{Kitano2019}, so detectors applied to UAV imagery have been used to count crops such as corn \cite{Kitano2019,Osco2021} and, for pineapple, to locate and count mature fruits \cite{Shiu2023}. Counting can be built on detection, by counting the detected boxes or segmented instances \cite{Machefer2020,Bumbaca2025}, with detectors of the YOLO family, which made real-time detection possible \cite{Redmon2016} and have been adapted to aerial imagery \cite{Tang2024}, or by learning a density map \cite{Lempitsky2010,Ayalew2020}. This work does not train or evaluate a counting model: it studies the step that comes before it.

\subsection{Research line of the group}

This work belongs to a research line on the detection, tracking, and generation of pineapple imagery captured by drones, in a setting of scarce labels. On the detection side, Xie-Li et al.\ proposed a training-free few-shot framework, based on visual foundation models, to localize pineapples in drone images of large fields where labeled data are scarce and expensive \cite{XieLi2024}, and PineSORT tracks pineapples in drone video for yield estimation \cite{XieLi2025PineSORT}. Earlier work showed that a few labeled images and the Segment Anything Model are enough to build a detector for pineapple drone imagery that outperforms existing detectors \cite{Fallas2023}; that adding near-infrared and red-edge channels to the RGB images can help detection when the images are well aligned and early fusion is used, although the expected gain over RGB faced challenges \cite{Gonzalez2023MS}; and, more recently, that multimodal foundation models match or exceed classical detectors for pineapple counting with only 5 and 10 training images \cite{Fallas2026Count}. The quality of the pseudo-labels of semi-supervised detection, which tolerates few labeled samples, has also been studied \cite{Fallas2025Pseudo}. On the generation side, Gonz\'alez-Hern\'andez et al.\ trained a latent diffusion model from scratch on small pineapple datasets, and found that a continuous latent space (a $\beta$-VAE) preserved the structure of the crop where a discrete one (a VQ-VAE) failed, and that a purely convolutional U-Net avoided the instabilities caused by global attention \cite{Gonzalez2026}. Xie-Li et al.\ compared $\beta$-VAE (with several values of $\beta$) and VQ-VAE on about 4\,000 frames from a pineapple plantation of Upala Agr\'icola, using five-fold cross-validation; they evaluated the reconstruction and whether the latent space separates the plants from the background, and found that an intermediate $\beta$ ($0.001$) gave the best balance between reconstruction and latent structure \cite{XieLi2025VAE}. That study did not evaluate the generation of images.

The present work starts from the recipe of \cite{Gonzalez2026} and asks how to improve it systematically through the data and the structure of the VAE. The data preparation and the evaluation differ. That study used about 5\,000 frames extracted from videos recorded at a two-meter altitude, with a VAE that takes three color channels, and evaluated the generations with the Fr\'echet Distance after DDIM sampling \cite{Gonzalez2026}. This work uses 4\,908 frames of $960\times544$ pixels (reduced from $1\,920\times1\,088$) with an estimated depth channel, a split by capture blocks, and FID and KID computed on a number of generated images larger than the 400 of the preliminary experiments, chosen in Stage~0 (Chapter~IV). For this reason, the values reported here are not directly comparable with those of that study.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

This section presents, in sequential order, the concepts needed to follow the rest of the proposal: how an image becomes a tensor, how a neural network learns, how a convolutional network processes images, and how these pieces are combined into the autoencoders and the generative models studied in this work.

\subsection{From pixels to tensors}

A digital image is a grid of pixels. Each pixel stores one intensity value per channel: a color image has three channels (red, green, and blue), and each value is usually an 8-bit integer between 0 and 255. The dataset of this work contains aerial frames of $544\times960$ pixels, and each frame has an associated depth map, a single channel whose values are normalized per image.

In PyTorch \cite{Paszke2019}, an image is represented as a \emph{tensor}, a multidimensional array of numbers that can be moved to a GPU and differentiated automatically. The convention is channels first: an image has shape $(C,H,W)$, and a batch of $N$ images has shape $(N,C,H,W)$. Before training, the integers are converted to floating-point numbers and divided by 255, so that every value lies in $[0,1]$. \subsection{Neural networks and learning}

A neural network is a composition of layers, $f_\theta=f_L\circ\dots\circ f_1$, where each layer applies a linear map followed by a nonlinear function, and $\theta$ denotes all the learnable parameters. Learning consists of minimizing a loss function $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ that measures how far the output is from the desired result. The gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter is computed by backpropagation \cite{Rumelhart1986,Goodfellow2016}, and the parameters are updated in the direction that reduces the loss. Optimizers such as Adam \cite{Kingma2015} and AdamW \cite{Loshchilov2019} adapt the size of each update, which is controlled by the \emph{learning rate}. The learning rate is often changed during training with a schedule, for example a cosine decay \cite{Loshchilov2017}; a poorly configured schedule can make training oscillate or become unstable. To detect overfitting, the data are split into training, validation, and test sets, and the model is selected with the validation loss.

\subsection{Convolutional neural networks}

A convolutional neural network (CNN) \cite{LeCun1998} processes images with filters that slide over the input and share their weights. For an input $X$ with $C$ channels, a convolutional layer with kernels $W$ and bias $b$ produces
\begin{equation}
Y_{c',i,j}=\sum_{c=1}^{C}\sum_{u,v}W_{c',c,u,v}\,X_{c,\,i+u,\,j+v}+b_{c'},
\end{equation}
which makes the layer sensitive to local patterns, such as the edge of a leaf, wherever they appear in the image. A stride of 2 halves the height and the width, so stacking such layers compresses the image spatially while increasing the number of channels. Residual blocks \cite{He2016} add the input of a block to its output to ease the training of deep networks, and group normalization \cite{Wu2018} stabilizes the activations. The U-Net \cite{Ronneberger2015} combines a downsampling path, an upsampling path, and skip connections between them; it is the architecture used by the generators of this work, in its convolutional version without attention layers.

\subsection{Autoencoders and the latent space}

An autoencoder is a network with two parts: an encoder $E$ that compresses an input $x$ into a smaller representation $z=E(x)$, called the \emph{latent}, and a decoder $D$ that reconstructs the input, $\hat{x}=D(z)$. It is trained to minimize the reconstruction error. In this work, the encoder maps a $(4,256,256)$ tensor to a latent of shape $(4,32,32)$: a grid of $32\times32$ positions, each with 4 values. Each position is called a \emph{token}, and for this reason the autoencoder is also referred to as a tokenizer in the literature.

\subsection{Variational autoencoders}

A variational autoencoder (VAE) \cite{Kingma2014} learns a probabilistic encoder $q_\phi(z\mid x)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(x),\sigma_\phi^2(x))$ and a decoder $p_\theta(x\mid z)$. It is trained by minimizing a reconstruction term plus a regularization term that keeps the latent close to a standard Gaussian prior:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VAE}} = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[\lVert x-\hat{x}\rVert_2^2\big] + \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\Vert\,\mathcal{N}(0,I)\big),
\end{equation}
where the latent is sampled with the reparameterization $z=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$. The weight $\beta$ controls the trade-off between reconstruction and regularization \cite{Higgins2017}; in this work $\beta=10^{-3}$. A discrete alternative replaces the Gaussian latent with a codebook of embeddings (VQ-VAE) \cite{vandenOord2017}. One of the structural modifications studied in this work is a \emph{variational output layer}: instead of producing the image directly, the last layer of the decoder predicts a mean and a log-variance for every output value and the image is sampled from that distribution.

\subsection{Data treatments}

A data treatment is any transformation applied to the training data before it reaches the model. Data augmentation applies random transformations that preserve the content of the image; for aerial imagery, which has no canonical orientation, these include rotations by multiples of $90^{\circ}$, flips, and random crops. Random masking hides random regions of the image so that the model must infer the missing content; masked autoencoders \cite{He2022} reconstruct the hidden patches, and masking has also been used to train tokenizers for diffusion models \cite{Chen2025}. A third treatment is image selection, in which the training set is changed by choosing or repeating certain images, for example those with the largest reconstruction error.

\subsection{Adversarial training}

A generative adversarial network (GAN) \cite{Goodfellow2014} trains a discriminator $D_\psi$ to distinguish real from generated images while the generator is trained to fool it. When the discriminator judges local patches instead of the whole image \cite{Isola2017}, it encourages sharp local texture. In a VAE, an adversarial term can be added to the loss, $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VAE}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adv}}$, with a small weight $\lambda$ (here $\lambda=0.01$).

\subsection{Latent diffusion, DDPM, and Flow Matching}

A latent diffusion model (LDM) \cite{Rombach2022} separates image generation into two stages: an autoencoder compresses the images into latents, and a generative model is trained in the latent space, so that new latents can be sampled and decoded into images. Working in the latent space reduces the cost of training and sampling with respect to diffusion in pixel space.

A denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) \cite{Ho2020} defines a forward process that gradually adds Gaussian noise to a latent $z_0$,
\begin{equation}
z_t=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,z_0+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}\,\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),
\end{equation}
and trains a network $\varepsilon_\theta$ to predict the added noise,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DDPM}}=\mathbb{E}_{z_0,\varepsilon,t}\big[\lVert\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta(z_t,t)\rVert_2^2\big].
\end{equation}
Sampling starts from pure noise and removes it step by step (here, with 1000 training steps). Flow Matching (FM) \cite{Lipman2023} instead learns a velocity field along a path between noise $x_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and data $x_1$. With the linear path $x_t=(1-t)\,x_0+t\,x_1$, the target velocity is constant, $v=x_1-x_0$, and the loss is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}=\mathbb{E}_{x_0,x_1,t}\big[\lVert v_\theta(x_t,t)-(x_1-x_0)\rVert_2^2\big].
\end{equation}
Sampling integrates the learned velocity with a few Euler steps (here, 50), which makes generation considerably faster than DDPM sampling.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}

The quality of the reconstruction is measured with the mean squared error (MSE) and the peak signal-to-noise ratio. The quality of the generated images is measured by comparing the distribution of generated images with that of real images in the feature space of an Inception network. The Fr\'echet Inception Distance (FID) \cite{Heusel2017} models both sets of features as Gaussians with means $\mu_r,\mu_g$ and covariances $\Sigma_r,\Sigma_g$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FID}=\lVert\mu_r-\mu_g\rVert_2^2+\mathrm{Tr}\!\big(\Sigma_r+\Sigma_g-2(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}\big).
\end{equation}
The Kernel Inception Distance (KID) \cite{Binkowski2018} is the squared maximum mean discrepancy with a polynomial kernel; unlike FID, it has an unbiased estimator and does not depend on the number of generated images in the same way, so it is more reliable for small samples. For both metrics, lower is better.

\section{Related Work}

The literature closest to this work is organized in five themes: the design of the latent space, the treatments of the training data of the autoencoder, the generators (diffusion and FM), generation for small datasets in agriculture, and multimodal inputs. The search that supports the claim of novelty is described in the last subsection.

\subsection{Design of the Latent Space of Latent Diffusion Models}

Latent diffusion models \cite{Rombach2022} separate compression and generation, so the autoencoder sets the ceiling of what the generator can learn. The design of that latent space has been studied mostly with large benchmarks, and its main lesson is that reconstruction and generation do not go together. A larger number of channels per token improves the reconstruction but demands larger diffusion models and longer training to reach a comparable generation \cite{Yao2025}; latent spaces with excessive high-frequency components hinder the diffusion process \cite{Skorokhodov2025}; a better reconstruction of the tokenizer does not necessarily translate into a better generation \cite{Zhong2026}; and, under a limited training budget, a higher-dimensional latent space even degraded the generation \cite{Sehwag2025}. Lee et al.\ identify latent smoothness, perceptual compression quality, and reconstruction quality as the properties that an autoencoder needs for latent diffusion, and show that existing autoencoders do not satisfy the three at once \cite{Lee2025LDMAE}. Chen et al.\ tie the quality of generation to the structure of the latent distribution, and find that the variational form of the autoencoder is not necessary \cite{Chen2025}. These studies motivate evaluating an autoencoder by the generation it enables and not only by its reconstruction, which is the approach of this work, but they are mostly evaluated on large datasets.

\subsection{Treatments of the Training Data of the Autoencoder}

Masked autoencoders reconstruct the hidden patches of an image, and masking a high proportion of the input, for example 75\%, is a nontrivial and meaningful self-supervised task \cite{He2022}. Masking has also been used to train tokenizers for diffusion models: MAETok uses mask modeling to obtain a semantically rich latent space while keeping the reconstruction fidelity \cite{Chen2025}, and the variational masked autoencoders of Lee et al.\ build on masked autoencoders and improve the quality of generation in latent diffusion \cite{Lee2025LDMAE}. These works are mostly evaluated on large datasets. According to the literature reviewed, the effect of other treatments, such as augmentation or the selection of images, on the generation from small datasets has been measured only in isolated cases: Fallah and Herzog report that the preprocessing of a small cohort of 295 participants changes the KID of the images generated by a latent diffusion model \cite{Fallah2026}.

\subsection{Diffusion and Flow Matching Generators}

Denoising diffusion probabilistic models learn to reverse a gradual noising process and need many sampling steps \cite{Ho2020}; denoising diffusion implicit models keep the same training and sample 10 to 50 times faster \cite{Song2021}. FM trains continuous normalizing flows by regressing the vector fields of fixed conditional probability paths, without simulating the flow; with diffusion paths, it is a more robust and stable way to train diffusion models, and it also allows other paths, such as those of optimal transport \cite{Lipman2023}. Rectified flow learns ODEs that follow straight paths between noise and data, which can be simulated without time discretization and yield computationally efficient models \cite{Liu2023}. At scale, a rectified flow formulation outperformed established diffusion formulations in text-to-image synthesis \cite{Esser2024}, and scalable interpolant transformers surpassed diffusion transformers at every size with the same architecture, parameters, and computation \cite{Ma2024}. In agriculture, a FM model for pairs of pest and disease images and masks reached a lower FID than GAN and diffusion comparators on four datasets, and its generated images improved the segmentation of two networks \cite{Kong2026}. These results come from large-scale settings or from tasks different from the aerial imagery of this work. The work of the group on pineapple used DDPM with DDIM sampling \cite{Gonzalez2026}; this work trains DDPM and FM on the same latent spaces, which allows to check whether an effect depends on the generator.

\subsection{Generation for Small Datasets in Agriculture and Aerial Imagery}

Limited annotated data and imbalanced samples are a recurrent problem in agricultural imaging, and diffusion models have been reviewed as a way to address it \cite{Hu2025}. Generated images have been used to enrich the training data for plant disease \cite{Muhammad2023}, vineyard shoot detection \cite{Hirahara2025}, and the detection and counting of sorghum panicles in UAV images \cite{Cai2022}. For pineapple, the group trained a latent diffusion model from scratch on small UAV datasets and showed that a continuous latent space and a convolutional U-Net give a stable training \cite{Gonzalez2026}, and compared variants of variational autoencoders for the same imagery in terms of reconstruction and latent structure \cite{XieLi2025VAE}. The first of these compares one structural choice, a continuous against a discrete latent space. According to the search described in the last subsection, none of them varies the treatments of the data or evaluates several modifications of the VAE on the quality of the generated images, which is the question of this work.

\subsection{Multimodal Inputs: RGB and Depth}

Agricultural imagery is increasingly multimodal. Orchid learns a joint appearance and geometry prior: a VAE encodes RGB, relative depth, and surface normals into a shared latent space, and a latent diffusion model denoises it \cite{Krishnan2025}. In pineapple crops, the group studied the addition of near-infrared and red-edge channels to the RGB images for object detection, and compared early and late fusion \cite{Gonzalez2023MS}. Giving the depth channel as an input of the VAE is an early fusion, and it is one of the factors of this work.

\textbf{Screening criteria.} Records were screened by title and abstract with three criteria:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{C1:} the work varies something in the autoencoder or in its training data (augmentation, masking, adversarial loss, KL or regularization, number of latent channels, or fine-tuning).
  \item \textbf{C2:} the work measures generation (FID, KID, generation or sample quality, image generation or synthesis).
  \item \textbf{C3:} the work addresses few or homogeneous data (small or limited datasets, few images, scarcity, few-shot, data efficiency) or an aerial, agricultural, or medical domain.
\end{enumerate}
The screening was automatic: each criterion was a set of keywords applied to the title and abstract. For the Semantic Scholar records that had not been found on arXiv, the same criteria were re-implemented as keyword rules from their written definition, because the first script was not kept; the records found in both sources kept their arXiv screening. The titles of the records that met C1, C2, and C3 were reviewed, and the abstracts of the closest ones were read. A work is considered to study the review question when it meets the three criteria and examines how the data or the structure of the autoencoder change generation.

\textbf{Result.} Figure~\ref{fig:prisma} summarizes the flow, which follows the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram \cite{Page2021}. The two sources returned 442 records, of which 258 were unique. Of these, 84 met C1 and C2, and 36 also met C3 or the domain criterion. Most of the 36 are medical applications that use a VAE to synthesize data; the others are spectral data (soil, water quality) and a multimodal remote sensing generator with a domain-specific VAE trained on 11.5 million images, a large-scale setting. Two works come closest to the review question, both in medical imaging. Fallah and Herzog report a controlled ablation of the preprocessing of a small cohort (295 participants) and measure its effect on the KID and FID of the images generated by a VAE and a latent diffusion model \cite{Fallah2026}; Dombrowski et al.\ show that the structure of the latent space of pretrained autoencoders, rather than their fidelity, limits the usefulness of synthetic medical images \cite{Dombrowski2026}. According to their abstracts, neither studies aerial or agricultural images, multimodal inputs, or structural modifications of the VAE. Therefore, among the records retrieved by these searches, none studied the review question. The preprocessing ablation of Fallah and Herzog shows that the effect of a data treatment on generation has been measured at small-cohort scale, so, up to the date of the search, the claim of novelty applies to the combination of data treatments, VAE modifications, and the aerial, agricultural, multimodal regime.

\textbf{Complementary searches.} The references on plant counting, object detection, data scarcity, and synthetic data used in the Background were located with 14 targeted queries on Semantic Scholar (Academic Graph API, 18 October 2026, 20 results per query, publications from 2012 onward); the title, authors, venue, and abstract of every work cited from them were checked on the same source. Four further queries that combine Flow Matching with agriculture, small datasets, and aerial imagery were run on the same source and date; they returned one FM model for agricultural pest and disease images \cite{Kong2026} and no study of FM as a generator on aerial imagery with few data among the results retrieved. These searches support the narrative of the Background and the Related Work, and they are not part of the PRISMA flow above, which answers the review question only.

\textbf{Limitations.} Only arXiv and Semantic Scholar were searched, the screening relied on keywords (re-implemented for the second source), there was no registered protocol, and the search was not repeated by a second reviewer. PRISMA 2020 is followed only in how the flow is reported; this is a first systematic search and not a full systematic review.
